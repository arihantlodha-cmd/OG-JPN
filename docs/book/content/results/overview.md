# Policy results

Three fiscal reforms that Japan is actually debating, each solved on Japan's demographics as a long-run steady-state comparison and on the transition path.

| Reform | Long-run effect |
|---|---|
| {ref}`Consumption tax 10 to 15 percent <Chap_ConsTax>` | revenue up about 1.5 points of GDP per two-point rise; consumption down, labor slightly up |
| {ref}`Pension age 65 to 70 <Chap_RetAge>` | pension outlays from 11.5 to 9.6 percent of GDP; labor up |
| {ref}`Replacement-rate cut of 10 and 20 percent <Chap_ReplRate>` | pension outlays from 11.5 to 9.2 percent of GDP; labor up |

## What the transition adds

In all three reforms, the transition path shows **capital building up** over the horizon, as each reform tilts households toward saving. The steady-state comparison alone does not show this; for the pension-age and replacement-rate reforms it even suggests the opposite. That is what the dynamics are for.

## How to read the numbers

The tax side of the calibration is first-pass (see {ref}`Chap_Taxes`), so read results for their direction and rough size rather than their last digit. Reform-minus-baseline comparisons are robust to the model's level misses in `C/Y` and household wealth, because the baseline and the reform carry the same calibration. The single-period resource-constraint errors at t = 2 and t = 120 (see {ref}`Chap_Wealth` and {ref}`Chap_Demog`) are common to baseline and reform and cancel in the differences.

The published results were produced with OG-Core's default initial wealth distribution. Rerunning them from Japan's observed distribution ({ref}`Chap_Wealth`) is on the roadmap.
