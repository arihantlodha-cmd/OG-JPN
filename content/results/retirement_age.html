Raising the pension eligibility age#

Japan’s public pension starts at 65. This reform raises the eligibility age to 68 and to 70, as several aging economies have done or are debating.

Each reform is solved two ways on Japan’s demographics: as a comparison of long-run steady states, and on the full transition path, which shows how the economy moves year by year and gives the net present value of the change. Regenerate them with examples/analysis_retirement_age.py and examples/analysis_retirement_age_tpi.py.

What this shows#

Raising the age at which households can claim the public pension does what the fiscal debate expects, on Japan’s real age structure. People work longer, so labor rises (and by more at the higher age); the pension bill falls directly, from 12.0 percent of GDP to 9.9 at age 70, because each cohort collects for fewer years. Capital edges down rather than up: with a shorter retirement to fund, households need less lifecycle wealth, so the saving they do over a working life buys a smaller capital stock. More labor and less capital roughly offset, leaving output close to flat, so the reform reads mainly as moving the adjustment onto the spending side of the budget rather than as a growth lever. The clean result is the pension line: moving the age to 70 takes 2.1 points of GDP off the public pension burden in the long run.

The magnitudes are indicative, not final. This is a comparison of two steady states, robust to the level miss in C/Y (see the validation chapter of the docs) because both sides carry the same calibration, so the honest reading is the direction and rough size, not the last digit. The transition-path version (results_retirement_age_tpi.md) adds the year-by-year phase-in.

What this adds over the steady state#

The steady-state comparison found labor up, the pension bill down, and capital slightly lower. The transition traces the path on Japan’s aging population. The pension bill is the headline a finance ministry watches: under the age-70 reform it is 2.1 points of GDP below the baseline after 10 years and 2.3 after 20, and the NPV table above discounts that saving over the first decade. By year 20, capital is +2.6 percent under the age-68 reform and +4.6 percent under 70, and output +2.8 percent under 70.

Where the capital comes from. OG-Core holds government purchases at a fixed share of GDP for the first 20 years, so the budget gain from the move to a pension age of 70 goes to paying down debt: by year 20, debt is 1.07 times GDP against 1.62 in the baseline. Lower debt frees household saving for private capital. That, not extra household saving, is what lifts capital: household wealth is 1.6 percent lower (households save less), while capital is +4.6 percent at year 20. After year 20 OG-Core’s closure rule brings debt back to its target, and capital ends the path -1.3 percent from baseline. Most of the capital gain along the transition is therefore a consequence of using the proceeds to pay down debt, as OG-Core’s fiscal closure does.

The magnitudes are indicative, not final, so read the shape of the paths and the sign of the NPV, not the last digit. The reported quantities are reform-minus-baseline differences, in which the single-period resource-constraint errors at t=2 and t=120 cancel.
