Cutting the pension replacement rate#

Japan’s macroeconomic slide (マクロ経済スライド) automatically trims benefit growth below wage and price growth, lowering the replacement rate over time. This reform cuts the replacement rate by 10 and 20 percent.

Each reform is solved two ways on Japan’s demographics: as a comparison of long-run steady states, and on the full transition path, which shows how the economy moves year by year and gives the net present value of the change. Regenerate them with examples/analysis_replacement_rate.py and examples/analysis_replacement_rate_tpi.py.

What this shows#

Cutting the replacement rate is the other half of the pension debate from the retirement-age experiment: it changes how much retirees receive rather than when they can claim, and it produces the same signature. The direct effect is mechanical, the pension bill falls roughly in proportion to the cut (a ten percent cut takes it from about 12.1 to 10.9 percent of GDP, a twenty percent cut to 9.7). The behavioral response runs mainly through labor: facing a thinner public pension, households work more (labor up about half a percent at the ten percent cut and over one percent at twenty), which lifts output modestly. Capital edges down rather than up in this long-run comparison of steady states, the same pattern the retirement-age reform showed, so the reform is best read as pushing people to work more and trimming the pension bill, not as a saving-and-capital story. Its distributional cost falls on retirees rather than workers, the mirror image of raising the claiming age.

The magnitudes are indicative, not final. This is a comparison of two steady states, robust to the level miss in C/Y (see docs/METHODOLOGY.md) because both sides carry the same calibration, but the pension calibration is still first-pass, so the honest reading is the direction and rough size, not the last digit. The retirement-age transition showed capital building up over the path even where its steady-state comparison did not, so the transition version of this reform is the natural next step.

What this adds over the steady state#

The steady-state comparison found the pension bill down, labor up, and capital slightly lower. The transition traces the path on Japan’s aging population. Under the 20% cut the pension bill is 2.2 points of GDP below the baseline after 10 years and 2.5 after 20, and the NPV table above discounts that saving over the first decade. Labor rises and stays up. By year 20, capital is +3.1 percent under the 10% cut and +6.3 percent under 20%, and output +3.7 percent under 20%.

Where the capital comes from. OG-Core holds government purchases at a fixed share of GDP for the first 20 years, so the budget gain from the 20% benefit cut goes to paying down debt: by year 20, debt is 0.98 times GDP against 1.59 in the baseline. Lower debt frees household saving for private capital. That, not extra household saving, is what lifts capital: household wealth is 1.0 percent lower (households save less), while capital is +6.3 percent at year 20. After year 20 OG-Core’s closure rule brings debt back to its target, and capital ends the path -1.1 percent from baseline. Most of the capital gain along the transition is therefore a consequence of using the proceeds to pay down debt, as OG-Core’s fiscal closure does.

The magnitudes are indicative, not final, so read the shape of the paths and the sign of the NPV, not the last digit. The reported quantities are reform-minus-baseline differences, in which the single-period resource-constraint errors at t=2 and t=120 cancel.
