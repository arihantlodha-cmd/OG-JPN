Cutting the pension replacement rate#

Japan’s macroeconomic slide (マクロ経済スライド) automatically trims benefit growth below wage and price growth, lowering the replacement rate over time. This reform cuts the replacement rate by 10 and 20 percent.

Each reform is solved two ways on Japan’s demographics: as a comparison of long-run steady states, and on the full transition path, which shows how the economy moves year by year and gives the net present value of the change. Regenerate them with examples/analysis_replacement_rate.py and examples/analysis_replacement_rate_tpi.py.

What this shows#

Cutting the replacement rate is the other half of the pension debate from the retirement-age experiment: it changes how much retirees receive rather than when they can claim, and it produces the same signature. The direct effect is mechanical, the pension bill falls roughly in proportion to the cut (a ten percent cut takes it from about 11.4 to 10.3 percent of GDP, a twenty percent cut to 9.1). The behavioral response runs mainly through labor: facing a thinner public pension, households work more (labor up about half a percent at the ten percent cut and over one percent at twenty), which lifts output modestly. Capital edges down rather than up in this long-run comparison of steady states, the same pattern the retirement-age reform showed, so the reform is best read as pushing people to work more and trimming the pension bill, not as a saving-and-capital story. Its distributional cost falls on retirees rather than workers, the mirror image of raising the claiming age.

The magnitudes are indicative, not final. This is a comparison of two steady states, robust to the level miss in C/Y (see docs/METHODOLOGY.md) because both sides carry the same calibration, but the pension calibration is still first-pass, so the honest reading is the direction and rough size, not the last digit. The retirement-age transition showed capital building up over the path even where its steady-state comparison did not, so the transition version of this reform is the natural next step.

What this adds over the steady state#

The steady-state comparison found the pension bill down, labor up, and capital slightly lower. The transition traces the path on Japan’s real, aging age structure and, as with the retirement-age reform, flips the read on capital. The pension outlay drops year by year as the thinner benefit takes hold, several tenths of a point of GDP below the baseline throughout, and the NPV table above discounts that saving over the first decade. Labor rises strongly and stays up. And capital, which the steady-state end-point comparison showed slightly lower, builds up over the horizon instead (about +3% by year 20 under the 10% cut and +6% under 20%): facing a thinner public pension, households save more of their higher labor income during the transition, so the capital stock accumulates and output climbs with it. This is the crowding-in the steady-state levels alone did not show, and it is the same transition feature the consumption-tax and retirement-age reforms displayed.

One reading caveat near the start: under the 10% cut the year-1 consumption figure is an outlier, reflecting the abrupt retiming of consumption plus the initial-condition transient noted in the module docstring; read it as noise. The magnitudes are indicative, not final (the pension calibration is still first-pass), so read the shape of the paths and the sign of the NPV, not the last digit. The reported quantities are reform-minus-baseline differences, in which the single-period t=2 initial-condition artifact cancels.
