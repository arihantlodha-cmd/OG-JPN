Demographics#

Japan’s demographics are the reason to build this model. OG-JPN takes them directly from the UN World Population Prospects through OG-Core’s demographics module, with no Japan-specific adjustment.

Source#

ogjpn.calibrate.Calibration calls ogcore.demographics.get_pop_objs with:

Argument

Value

country_id

"392" (Japan’s UN M49 code)

initial_data_year / final_data_year

start year - 1 / start year + 1 (start year 2025)

ages

0 to 99, mapped to the model’s E = 20 and S = 80

income_percentiles

OG-Core’s lifetime-income group shares (lambdas), so the population arrays are J wide

OG-Core uses the UN fertility, mortality, and population data to build the population distribution by age (omega), population growth (g_n), mortality (rho), and immigration rates along the transition path and in the steady state.

Japan’s UN data are in the shared EAPD-DRB/Population-Data mirror (added by this project in Population-Data #14), and Japan is in OG-Core’s country_dict (OG-Core #1196), so the model runs offline.

What the data imply#

Japan’s population is shrinking: the steady-state population growth rate is about -1.07 percent a year. The age distribution in Fig. 1 shows the shift toward old age between 1990 and 2030.

This one input explains much of Japan’s pension burden. Holding OG-Core’s default (US) pension formula fixed, swapping in Japan’s demographics raises steady-state pension outlays from about 8 to 9 percent of GDP to about 12 percent. With Japan’s own pension formula (Public pensions and the yen anchor), first-year outlays are 10.2 percent of GDP against Japan’s 10.4.

Known limitations#

  • Common mortality across income groups. Every lifetime-income group faces the same mortality. Japan’s income-mortality gradient is not yet modeled; EAPD-DRB’s Demographic-Gradients project is the natural route, but its rules are built for developing countries and need Japan-specific data.

  • The population is fixed after 120 periods. OG-Core holds the population distribution constant from period fixper = 1.5 * S (120). Japan’s age distribution is still moving at that point (about 0.5 percent a period, L1 norm), so the freeze shows up as a small jump in population growth (-1.096 to -1.070 percent) and a one-period resource-constraint error of about 1.2e-3 at t = 120. It does not affect steady-state results.
